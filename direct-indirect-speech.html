The Witness Has Something to Say

🔴 LIVE COVERAGE
"We are live outside City Hall, where the Mayor has just addressed the crowd. Dozens of witnesses saw what happened — but how do we tell our readers exactly what was said? That's where Direct and Indirect Speech become our most essential tools."

As a news reporter, you will constantly need to include quotes from interviews, eyewitnesses, and officials. You have two choices: copy their exact words, or report what they said in your own sentence structure.

📢 Direct Speech
The Mayor said, "We will fix the roads by Friday."
Exact words, inside quotation marks.
📝 Indirect Speech
The Mayor said that they would fix the roads by Friday.
Reported words, tense shifted back.

Over the next six slides, you will learn the rules for both types of speech, when journalists prefer each one, and how to convert between them. Let's go to press.

02 / 07 — Grammar Basics
Grammar Desk

The Rules of Reporting

Converting Direct → Indirect speech requires four key changes. Think of them as your editorial checklist before you file your report.

  • 1
    Add a reporting verb + "that" Remove the quotation marks and comma. Add a reporting verb (said, told, explained, announced, claimed, warned…) followed by that.
    Example
    Direct: He said, "I am tired."
    Indirect: He said that he was tired.
  • 2
    Backshift the tense When the reporting verb is past tense (said, told), shift all verbs one step further into the past. See the tense table below.
  • 3
    Change time & place expressions Words like now → then, today → that day, here → there, tomorrow → the next day, yesterday → the day before.
  • 4
    Shift pronouns First person (I, we, my) and second person (you, your) often change depending on who is speaking and who is listening.

⏱ Tense Backshift Table

Direct Speech (original) → Indirect Speech (reported)
present simple: am / is / are→past simple: was / were
present continuous: am going→past continuous: was going
present perfect: have seen→past perfect: had seen
past simple: arrived→past perfect: had arrived
will: will send→would: would send
can: can help→could: could help
must / have to→had to

03 / 07 — The Journalist's Choice
Style Guide

When to Quote, When to Report

Professional journalists choose between direct and indirect speech strategically. Knowing which to use — and why — is the difference between an amateur article and a polished news piece.

📢 Use Direct Speech when…
  • The exact words are powerful or surprising
  • You want to show the speaker's personality or emotion
  • It is a key official statement (e.g. a verdict, a confession)
  • The wording itself is newsworthy
"I will never resign," the minister insisted.
📝 Use Indirect Speech when…
  • You want to summarise a long statement briefly
  • The exact wording isn't important
  • You are combining several quotes into one sentence
  • You want the article to flow more smoothly
The minister insisted that she would not resign.

📰 Journalism Tip
Real newspaper articles mix both types. A typical paragraph might introduce a quote indirectly, then use a direct quote for impact — just like a film builds to its key scene.

07 / 08 — Reverse the Report
🎙 Back to the Source

Indirect → Direct Speech

Now work the other way. You have been given a reported (indirect) speech sentence from a news article. Your job is to reconstruct what the speaker actually said — in their exact words, with correct punctuation and tense.

🔄 Reverse Conversion Checklist
Undo the tense backshift (past → present, would → will, had done → have done) · Restore first-person pronouns (he/she → I, they → we) · Change time expressions back (that day → today, the next day → tomorrow) · Add quotation marks and a comma before the reporting clause.
Crime Scene
The officer said that they had secured the area.
Press Conference
The mayor announced that she would hold a press conference the following day.
Science Desk
The researcher explained that they were still analysing the data at that moment.
Sports Report
The coach said that the team had trained very hard that week.

08 / 08 — The Word Bank
✍️ The Reporter's Vocabulary

Beyond "Said" — Reporting Verbs

Professional journalists rarely use said twice in a row. The verb you choose tells the reader how something was communicated — whether it was a calm statement, an urgent warning, or a firm denial. Match the verb to the moment.

📢 Neutral / Factual

Use when reporting a straightforward statement

statedreportednotedcommentedobservedtold reporters
The spokesperson stated that the investigation was ongoing.
⚠️ Warning / Urgent

Use when the speaker is alerting or cautioning

warnedurgedcautionedstressedinsisted
The doctor warned that conditions could worsen overnight.
📣 Announcing / Declaring

Use for official news or formal declarations

announceddeclaredconfirmedrevealeddisclosed
The committee confirmed that a new policy would be introduced.
🔍 Explaining / Clarifying

Use when a source is giving reasons or detail

explainedclarifiedpointed outhighlightedelaborated
The engineer explained that the fault had been caused by heavy rainfall.
❓ Questioning / Doubting

Use when a source is uncertain or sceptical

questioneddoubtedqueriedchallengeddisputed
The opposition leader challenged the government's account of events.
🚫 Denying / Refusing

Use when a source rejects or refuses something

deniedrejectedrefuseddismissedrebuffed
The suspect denied any involvement in the incident.
